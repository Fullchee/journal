# Goal terminology: values, goals, targets and plans

Scope: what behaviour-change science calls each level of a goal hierarchy, where it splits "goal" from "target" and "standard", and what the journal should call each level. Researched 2026-11-21.

Builds on [goal-hierarchy.md](goal-hierarchy.md) (why Goals nest), [goal-hierarchies.md](goal-hierarchies.md) (the evidence for nesting and for linking Habits) and [habit-tracker-best-practices.md](habit-tracker-best-practices.md) (Frequency and scoring). Those findings are linked, not repeated.

## Answer

- **Science calls every level a "goal"**: values-like goals at the top, concrete actions at the bottom. Levels differ by how abstract they are, not by type ([Austin & Vancouver 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.120.3.338); [Carver & Scheier 1998](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139174794)). A numeric weekly target is a **specific, low-level goal**, not a different thing.
- **"Target" and "standard" are roles, not levels.** A standard (or reference value) is what a goal is compared against. A target is the measurable part of a goal ([BCIO: set measurable behaviour goal](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ols4/ontologies/bcio/classes?obo_id=BCIO:007300)).
- **The split that matters is behaviour vs outcome.** "Run 3 times a week" is a behaviour goal; "lose 5 kg" is an outcome goal ([Michie et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12160-013-9486-6), BCTs 1.1 and 1.3). A Habit's Frequency is closer to an **action plan** (BCT 1.4) than to a goal.
- **SMART is management lore, not science.** It comes from a 1981 management magazine. A 2022 review found it has no theory base and can do harm ([Swann et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2021.2023608)). Open goals lift activity as much as SMART goals and feel better to inactive people ([Goddard et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2025.103018)).
- **Rigid numbers carry a known mood cost.** Hard goals are met less often, so they satisfy less ([Locke & Latham 2002](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705)). Being able to let a goal go predicts well-being ([Barlow et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/jopy.12492)), and a felt gap from your ideal self tracks dejection ([Higgins 1987](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.94.3.319)).
- **Recommendation: merge, but name the parts.** Keep one **Goal** concept. Let a Goal hold an optional **Target**: a measure, a period or deadline, and a direction. Show a Target as progress, never pass/fail, and make "adjust" and "let go" normal review outcomes.

## 1. Terms by theory

### Control theory (Carver & Scheier)

- **Goals are reference values** in nested feedback loops. Each loop compares what it senses with its reference value and acts to shrink the gap ([Carver & Scheier 1982](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.92.1.111)).
- **Three named levels**: **be goals** (the self you want to be, "system concepts"), **do goals** (programs, such as "go for a run") and **motor-control goals** (the movements themselves) ([Carver & Scheier 1998](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139174794), ch. 5).
- **A higher loop sets the reference value for the loop below.** "Be fit" sets "run today", which sets the stride.
- **Feelings track the rate of progress**, not the gap itself ([Carver & Scheier 1990](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.97.1.19)).

### Goal constructs (Austin & Vancouver 1996)

- **Definition**: goals are "internal representations of desired states", where states can be outcomes, events or processes ([Austin & Vancouver 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.120.3.338)).
- **One family, many names**: the review gathers standards, reference values, set points, ideals, current concerns, personal strivings, life tasks and personal projects as goal constructs from different fields.
- **Structure**: goals form hierarchies, from abstract ideals down to concrete actions. Theories differ in which level they study, not in kind.

### Goal-setting theory (Locke & Latham)

- **Definition**: a goal is "the object or aim of an action, for example, to attain a specific standard of proficiency, usually within a specified time limit" ([Locke & Latham 2002](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705)). So a number with a deadline is a goal here.
- **Specific, difficult goals** beat "do your best" on performance (same paper).
- **Learning vs performance goals**: on complex or new tasks, a goal to find strategies beats a goal for a score ([Winters & Latham 1996](https://doi.org/10.1177/1059601196212007); [Seijts & Latham 2001](https://doi.org/10.1002/job.70)).
- **Proximal vs distal goals**: near-term sub-goals build skill, confidence and interest better than one far-off goal ([Bandura & Schunk 1981](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.41.3.586)); they help most when paired with a learning goal ([Seijts & Latham 2001](https://doi.org/10.1002/job.70)).
- **Goals double as standards for self-satisfaction**: higher goals produce more output but less satisfaction, because they are met less often ([Locke & Latham 2002](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705)).
- **Superordinate goals** (abstract, long-term) also motivate, and work best alongside concrete subordinate goals ([Höchli et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01879)).

### Goal intentions vs implementation intentions (Gollwitzer)

- **Goal intention**: "I intend to reach Z." It names an end state.
- **Implementation intention**: "If situation Y arises, then I will do X." It names when, where and how ([Gollwitzer 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.54.7.493)).
- **Implementation intentions serve goal intentions.** They are plans, one level below the goal. Effect sizes are in [goal-hierarchies.md](goal-hierarchies.md#implementation-intentions-gollwitzer).

### Values vs goals (ACT)

- **Values** are "chosen qualities of purposive action that can never be obtained as an object but can be instantiated moment by moment" ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2005.06.006)).
- **Goals** are things that can be completed. A value gives a direction; a goal is a waypoint along it (same paper).
- **ACT's own word for acting on values is "committed action"**: building larger patterns of action linked to values (same paper).
- **Fit**: the journal's Goal ("directional, never done on a clock") matches ACT's _value_ more than ACT's _goal_.

### Personal strivings and personal projects

- **Personal strivings** (Emmons): what a person is characteristically trying to do, such as "trying to make others laugh". A striving can be met or not on any day and is never finished ([Emmons 1986](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.51.5.1058)).
- **Personal projects** (Little): extended sets of personally relevant action, such as "learn to swim". A project has a start and an end ([Little 1983](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013916583153002)).
- **Fit**: a striving is a direction (the journal's Goal). A project is a finite goal, often with a deadline (the owner's "run 80 km by December").

### Approach vs avoidance goals (Elliot)

- **Approach goals** aim at a good state; **avoidance goals** aim away from a bad one ([Elliot & Church 1997](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.72.1.218)).
- **Avoidance goals cost well-being**: students with more avoidance strivings and projects lost well-being over a semester, through less perceived progress ([Elliot, Sheldon & Church 1997](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167297239001)).
- **Fit**: a bad Habit's **limit** is an avoidance goal. Pair it with an approach Goal it serves ("calmer evenings", not only "under 2 drinks").

### Behaviour Change Technique Taxonomy v1, cluster 1 "Goals and planning"

From [Michie et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12160-013-9486-6) and the [BCT Taxonomy site](https://www.bct-taxonomy.com/):

| BCT                                               | What it is                                                                                                           |
| ------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1.1 Goal setting (behavior)                       | Set or agree a goal defined as the **behaviour** to achieve                                                          |
| 1.2 Problem solving                               | Find barriers and ways around them                                                                                   |
| 1.3 Goal setting (outcome)                        | Set or agree a goal defined as a **positive outcome** of the behaviour                                               |
| 1.4 Action planning                               | Plan the behaviour in detail, with at least one of context, frequency, duration or intensity; includes if-then plans |
| 1.5 Review behavior goal(s)                       | Review the behaviour goal and consider changing the goal or the strategy                                             |
| 1.6 Discrepancy between current behavior and goal | Point out the gap between what the person does and the goal                                                          |
| 1.7 Review outcome goal(s)                        | Review the outcome goal and consider changing it                                                                     |
| 1.8 Behavioral contract; 1.9 Commitment           | A signed agreement; an affirmed commitment                                                                           |

- **Key point**: the taxonomy separates the **goal** (what to achieve) from the **action plan** (when, where, how often). "Run Mon, Wed, Fri at 7 am" is 1.4. "Run 12 km a week" is 1.1.
- **Review is built in**: 1.5 and 1.7 both expect the goal itself to change in light of progress.

### Behaviour Change Intervention Ontology (BCIO)

The BCIO turns the taxonomy into defined classes ([Michie et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.12688/wellcomeopenres.15902.2); [Marques et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.12688/wellcomeopenres.19363.2)). Definitions from the [BCIO in the EBI Ontology Lookup Service](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ols4/ontologies/bcio):

| Term                          | BCIO definition                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Goal                          | "A cognitive representation of an end state towards which one is striving" ([BCIO:006049](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ols4/ontologies/bcio/classes?obo_id=BCIO:006049))                                           |
| Behavioural goal              | "A goal in which the end state is a behaviour or behavioural pattern" ([BCIO:050937](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ols4/ontologies/bcio/classes?obo_id=BCIO:050937))                                                |
| Set measurable behaviour goal | Describes "the behaviour to be achieved in terms of a measurable target" ([BCIO:007300](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ols4/ontologies/bcio/classes?obo_id=BCIO:007300))                                             |
| Set outcome goal              | "The goal is a positive outcome of performing the behaviour" ([BCIO:007005](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ols4/ontologies/bcio/classes?obo_id=BCIO:007005))                                                         |
| Action planning               | Planning with a goal "specified in terms of some behaviour and a time or place where the behaviour is to be enacted" ([BCIO:050844](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ols4/ontologies/bcio/classes?obo_id=BCIO:050844)) |
| Outcome behaviour             | "Human behaviour that is an intervention outcome" ([BCIO:002000](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ols4/ontologies/bcio/classes?obo_id=BCIO:002000))                                                                    |
| Behavioural intention         | "A mental disposition to commit to enact or not enact a behaviour" ([BCIO:006016](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ols4/ontologies/bcio/classes?obo_id=BCIO:006016))                                                   |

- **"Target" is an attribute, not a class.** The BCIO has no "target" class. A measurable target is how a goal is described.
- **"Target behaviour"** is the behaviour an intervention tries to change; the BCIO's formal class for it is **outcome behaviour**. It is a researcher's term, not a person's goal.

## 2. Goal vs target vs standard

### In the science

- **Goal**: the desired end state, at any level of abstraction ([Austin & Vancouver 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.120.3.338); [BCIO:006049](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ols4/ontologies/bcio/classes?obo_id=BCIO:006049)).
- **Target**: the measurable description of a goal: a number, a unit and a period ([BCIO:007300](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ols4/ontologies/bcio/classes?obo_id=BCIO:007300)). "Target behaviour" is a separate, researcher-side term.
- **Standard / reference value**: the value a feedback loop compares against ([Carver & Scheier 1982](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.92.1.111)). Any goal plays this role while it is being pursued; a standard need not be conscious or chosen.
- **Outcome goal vs behaviour goal**: an outcome is a result of behaviour (weight, race time); a behaviour goal is the doing (km run). You control behaviour more directly ([Michie et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12160-013-9486-6), BCTs 1.1 vs 1.3).

### In common usage

- **"Goal"** often means a SMART, finishable goal.
- **"Target"** means a number to hit by a time, as in sales targets.
- **"Standard"** means a level to keep up over time, such as a fitness standard.
- **Practitioner splits**: OKRs call the direction an objective and the number a key result (see [section 3](#3-practitioner-frameworks-not-science)). Atomic Habits calls numbers "outcomes" and puts them below identity.

### Is SMART from the science?

- **No.** It traces to Doran (1981), "There's a S.M.A.R.T. way to write management's goals and objectives", a two-page piece in _Management Review_ 70(11). The letters vary between sources. _Flag_: Doran has no DOI or free copy; this citation comes from [Swann et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2021.2023608), a secondary source.
- **Goal-setting theory backs only parts of it**: specific and hard (not "achievable") ([Locke & Latham 2002](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705)). Locke and Latham did not derive SMART.
- **Critique**: SMART "is not based on scientific theory; is not consistent with empirical evidence; does not consider what type of goal is set … and has a risk of potentially harmful effects" ([Swann et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2021.2023608)).

### Evidence on numeric goals and mood

| Finding                                                                                                        | Source                                                                                                                 | Strength                      |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Open, do-your-best and SMART goals gave the same walking distance; open goals raised interest in more exercise | [Swann et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2020.1815100)                                                     | Small lab RCT (n = 82)        |
| Open and specific goals raised steps equally over 6 weeks; open goals felt better                              | [Goddard et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2025.103018)                                                | Pilot RCT (n = 30)            |
| Insufficiently active adults enjoyed open goals more than SMART; active adults preferred SMART                 | [Hawkins et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/1612197x.2025.2570187)                                                   | Small RCT plus interviews     |
| Higher goals give more output and less satisfaction                                                            | [Locke & Latham 2002](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705)                                                      | Review of many studies        |
| Side effects of goals: narrow focus, risk-taking, lost intrinsic motivation                                    | [Ordóñez et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.5465/AMP.2009.37007999)                                                       | Contested critique            |
| Letting go of unreachable goals, then re-engaging, goes with well-being                                        | [Wrosch et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167203256921)                                                         | 3 correlational studies       |
| Disengagement and re-engagement both predict quality of life; re-engagement more so                            | [Barlow et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/jopy.12492)                                                               | Meta-analysis, 31 samples     |
| In people at risk of depression, disengagement went with _more_ depressive symptoms                            | [Barlow et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/jopy.12492)                                                               | Same meta-analysis, moderator |
| Low mood helps people drop unreachable goals, which predicts fewer later symptoms                              | [Wrosch & Miller 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015172)                                                               | Longitudinal, adolescents     |
| Flexible goal adjustment buffers against depression; tenacious pursuit is a separate trait                     | [Brandtstädter & Renner 1990](https://doi.org/10.1037/0882-7974.5.1.58)                                                | Cross-sectional               |
| A gap between actual and ideal self tracks dejection and depression                                            | [Higgins 1987](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.94.3.319); [Strauman 1989](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-843X.98.1.14) | Theory plus clinical samples  |
| Depressed people hold as many goals but rate them less likely and less in their control                        | [Dickson, Moberly & Kinderman 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023665)                                                  | Small clinical sample         |
| A brief goal-setting and planning course raised well-being                                                     | [MacLeod et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10902-007-9057-2)                                                       | Small controlled trials       |

**Reading**: numbers are not the harm. The harm comes from a number that reads as a verdict on the self, with no easy way to adjust it. Disengagement helps in general, but for people prone to depression, dropping goals can be part of the low. So the app should make "adjust the Target" the default review move, with "let go" next to it and re-engaging with a new Target always offered.

## 3. Practitioner frameworks (not science)

None of these come from peer-reviewed research. They are useful vocabulary, not evidence.

| Framework                                                                                  | Its levels                                                                                                                                            | Maps to                                                            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **OKRs** ([What Matters](https://www.whatmatters.com/faqs/okr-meaning-definition-example)) | Objective: "what is to be achieved". Key results "benchmark and monitor how we get to the Objective", graded each quarter                             | Objective = Goal; key result = Target with a grade                 |
| **Atomic Habits** ([James Clear](https://jamesclear.com/identity-based-habits))            | Three layers: outcomes, processes, identity. Start from identity                                                                                      | Identity = be goal; process = Habit; outcome = outcome goal        |
| **Tiny Habits** ([BJ Fogg](https://behaviormodel.org/))                                    | Behavior = Motivation + Ability + Prompt. The book (Fogg 2020) splits aspirations, outcomes and behaviours, and writes recipes: "After I …, I will …" | Aspiration = Goal; recipe = implementation intention; prompt = Cue |
| **Yearly themes** ([Cortex's Theme System](https://www.thethemesystem.com/))               | One theme a year, such as "Year of Health", with no numbers                                                                                           | A top-level Goal, time-boxed                                       |
| **PARA areas** ([Tiago Forte](https://fortelabs.com/blog/para/))                           | Projects (finite) vs areas of responsibility: "important parts of your work and life that require ongoing attention"                                  | Area = Pillar or top Goal; project = Goal with a deadline          |

_Flag_: the Tiny Habits aspiration/outcome/behaviour split is from Fogg's book; his sites did not state it.

**Common thread**: every framework puts a direction above a measure. OKRs alone grade the measure.

## 4. Recommended vocabulary

### The levels

| Level       | Science term                                                    | Plain term           | Journal today                                    | Recommended                                    |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Life area   | Be goal; value (ACT); superordinate goal                        | Area, value          | **Pillar** (six fixed top-level Goals)           | Keep **Pillar**; a top-level Goal is its value |
| Direction   | Personal striving; do goal; outcome goal without a number       | Goal                 | **Goal**                                         | Keep **Goal**                                  |
| Finite goal | Personal project; distal goal                                   | Goal with a deadline | none                                             | **Goal** with a **Target** that has a deadline |
| Measure     | Specific or measurable goal; behaviour goal (BCT 1.1); standard | Target               | Signal target; Habit **Frequency** (minimum)     | **Target**, on a Goal                          |
| Plan        | Action plan (BCT 1.4); implementation intention                 | Plan                 | Habit **Frequency**, **Cue**, Places, usual time | Keep inside **Habit**                          |
| Habit       | Habit; target behaviour (researcher's word)                     | Habit                | **Habit**                                        | Keep **Habit**                                 |
| Action      | Motor-control goal; behaviour                                   | Action, log          | Check, Duration entry, One-off                   | Keep                                           |

### How a merged "Goal with optional measure" maps to the science

- **A Goal with no Target** is a value-like direction: ACT's value, Emmons's striving, Carver and Scheier's be goal.
- **A Goal with a Target** is goal-setting theory's specific goal. If the Target counts doing ("150 min of cardio a month"), it is a **behaviour goal**. If it counts a result ("resting heart rate under 60"), it is an **outcome goal**, and its number comes from a Signal.
- **A Target with a deadline** ("run 80 km by December") is a personal project: finite, then reviewed.
- **A weekly number on a Habit** is two things at once. "3 times a week" is when and how often: an **action plan**. "150 min a month" is an amount to reach: a **behaviour goal**. The science calls neither a key result; a key result is OKR's graded version of a behaviour or outcome goal.
- **"Standard"** is the role any Target plays while you track it. It is not a level and needs no entity.

### Recommendation

**Merge into one Goal concept with an optional Target.** Name the measured part **Target**, never "SMART goal".

- **Target holds**: a measure (minutes, km, times, or a linked Signal), an amount, a period (a week, a month) or a deadline, and a direction (at least or at most).
- **Habit Frequency stays the action plan.** It says when you plan to act. A Target says how much you aim to reach, summed from the Habits or Signal it links.
- **Show progress as rate, not pass/fail**: "54 of 80 km, on pace", and never a red miss. This follows control theory's rate-of-progress finding.
- **Make review the exit**: at the period's end or the deadline, offer **keep**, **adjust** or **let go**, as BCTs 1.5 and 1.7 describe. Letting go is a normal outcome, and the Goal above stays.
- **Prefer behaviour Targets.** Allow outcome Targets only through a Signal, since you control behaviour, not outcomes.
- **Pair limits with an approach Goal**, because avoidance-only goals lower well-being.

### Tradeoffs

| Option                                                        | Gains                                                                                                    | Costs                                                                                                                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Merge: Goal + optional Target** (recommended)               | One concept that matches the science and the owner's mental model. One home for numbers instead of three | Rewrites "directional, not SMART" in `GLOSSARY.md`. Brings back a number the depressive loop can latch on to, so the review and display rules carry the safety |
| Keep separate: ungraded Goal, Signal targets, Habit Frequency | Status quo. No grading risk at the Goal level                                                            | Numbers live in three places. "Run 80 km by December" has no home                                                                                              |
| Full OKRs: Goal + graded key results                          | Familiar from work                                                                                       | Grades each period, which the evidence above warns against. Quarterly cadence fits work, not life                                                              |
